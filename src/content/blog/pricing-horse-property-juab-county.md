Horse property in Juab County is priced off comparable sales like any other home, but there are fewer of them, they're spread across more miles, and the things horse buyers care about (zoning, water, access, outbuildings) don't show up in a square-footage number. So the price comes from careful adjustments and a clear explanation, not a quick average.

I'm a REALTOR®, and I'm also an active Utah Certified Residential Appraiser. That second job shapes how I look at these properties. Here's the process, in plain terms.

## Is the property actually horse-legal?

This is the first question, and I don't take the listing remarks' word for it. "Horse property" in an MLS listing is a marketing phrase. Whether a lot can legally keep horses, and how many, depends on the zoning and the animal-unit rules for that city or for unincorporated Juab County.

Nephi, Mona, Levan and the county each have their own code. Lot size, zone, setbacks from neighboring homes, and grandfathered uses can all change the answer. Two lots that look the same from the road can have different rules.

So before I put a price on anything sold as horse property, I check the code that actually applies to that parcel. If the use isn't allowed, the buyer pool shrinks, and so does the price a buyer will pay for the "horse" part.

## Do barns, arenas and fencing add their full cost to the price?

Usually not. A barn, an arena, good pipe fencing, automatic waterers and a tack room all add appeal. But buyers rarely pay dollar-for-dollar what those improvements cost to build.

Part of that is taste: one buyer wants an arena, the next wants a shop. Part of it is condition. A 30-year-old pole barn with a sagging roof is a project, not a feature. And part of it is that an improvement is only worth what the local buyer pool will pay for it, not what the receipt says.

When I price a property like this, I look at how the market has treated similar improvements on sales that actually closed, and I adjust from there. Just put in a new arena? I'll tell you straight what I think it adds. Usually less than you hope, more than nothing.

## Why are there so few comparable sales?

Because very few horse properties sell in any one Juab County town in a typical year, and some of the ones that do aren't good matches because of acreage, water, or the age and size of the home.

That means I often have to widen the search: older sales with a time adjustment, sales from Nephi, Mona and Levan together, and sometimes sales from southern Utah County or neighboring counties. Then I have to explain each difference: different town, different water, different setup. That's the same discipline I use on appraisal work. More on that in [how to price a rural property correctly](/blog/how-to-price-a-rural-property-correctly/).

Automated estimates struggle here. They can't tell which sales are truly comparable, or whether a lot can legally keep horses.

## Can water rights matter more than the barn?

Often, yes. Irrigation shares and water rights can move the price more than the barn does. Pasture without water is dry ground for most of the year out here. A property with irrigation shares that actually deliver water, and a way to get that water onto the pasture, is a different property than the one next door without them.

I want to know:

- How many shares, in which irrigation company, and are they tied to the parcel or sold separately?
- Is there a culinary water connection or a private well, and what does the well right allow?
- Is there stock water, and is it legal for the number of animals the buyer plans to keep?

Water questions are big enough that they get their own section on my [Utah land page](/land) and in my [rural buying guide](/buying-rural).

## What else do horse buyers look at?

The practical stuff:

- **Access.** Can a truck and a loaded horse trailer get in, turn around, and get out without backing down a lane? Is the road county-maintained or a private easement?
- **Trailer turnaround and parking.** Room to park a gooseneck matters more to this buyer than a third garage bay.
- **Outbuilding condition.** Roofs, doors, stall walls and electrical. Old buildings can be a plus or a tear-down cost.
- **Fencing and layout.** Where the pasture sits relative to the house, the barn and the water.

None of these has a fixed dollar figure. Each one gets weighed against what the comparable sales show.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if a Juab County property is zoned for horses?
Check the zoning and animal rules for that exact parcel with the city (Nephi, Mona or Levan) or with Juab County if it's unincorporated. Don't rely on the listing remarks. I verify this before I price or show a property as horse property.

### Does a new barn or arena raise the price by what it cost?
Rarely. Buyers like good horse improvements, but they usually pay less than the build cost. How much less depends on condition, layout and what similar sales in the area show.

### Why can't I just use a price per acre for horse property?
Because acreage has diminishing returns, and water, zoning, access and the improvements matter more than the raw acre count. A flat per-acre number gives a false sense of precision on rural ground.

### Do irrigation shares affect the price of horse property?
Yes, often a lot. Shares that reliably deliver water to the pasture can change what a buyer will pay more than a barn will. Confirm which company, how many shares, and whether they convey with the land.

Yes. As your REALTOR®, I'll pull the comparable sales, walk the property and give you a market opinion and a suggested list price range. It's not an appraisal, and I'll tell you how I got there.
