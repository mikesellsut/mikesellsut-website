One parcel comes with an underground domestic well right and deeded irrigation company shares that keep five acres of pasture green; the other is "dry dirt" where you cannot drill a well or turn a ditch gate until you track down and buy water on the open market.

## Why Do Two Identical 5-Acre Parcels Sell for Tens of Thousands of Dollars Apart?

On an online map or a quick drive-by in June, two five-acre fields outside Manti, Mt. Pleasant, or Mona can look identical. Both have mountain views, perimeter fencing, and a flat spot for a house and a shop.

Why does one close for tens of thousands of dollars more than the other? Because in rural Utah, land and water are often separate assets:

- **Parcel A (Wet Acreage):** Includes a transferrable underground water right approved for a domestic well *plus* enough irrigation company shares to water the remaining pasture or hay ground through a pressurized secondary riser or ditch turn.
- **Parcel B (Dry Acreage):** Sits outside municipal water lines, comes with zero irrigation shares, and lies in a groundwater basin where you have to purchase an existing water right and file a change application with the Utah Division of Water Rights before the county will even issue a building permit.

If a buyer has to spend months hunting down an available water right or irrigation shares, pay transfer fees, and drill a deep well before they can build or water animals, that cost and risk comes straight off what they are willing to pay for the dirt. ## What Is the Difference Between Irrigation Company Shares and a Utah Water Right?

People use "water rights" and "water shares" interchangeably at the fence line, but legally and practically they work differently when you are buying land with water shares in Utah:

### 1. Deeded Water Rights (Underground Wells or Surface Rights)
A **water right** is a state-administered right recorded with the **Utah Division of Water Rights**, identified by an area-and-number code (for example, `65-xxxx` in the Sanpete basin or `63-xxxx` in the Juab/Sevier area). It specifies:
- **Nature of use:** Domestic (indoor household use for one family), stock watering (a set number of livestock equivalent units), and irrigation (measured in irrigated acreage).
- **Point of diversion:** The exact surveyed coordinates where the well, spring, or stream diversion sits.
- **Place of use:** The legal parcel where that water can legally be used.

Unless a seller specifically reserves a deeded water right in the deed, appurtenant water rights recorded on the land typically pass with the warranty deed—though in practice, we always spell out the exact water right number on the Utah Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC) and Water Rights Addendum and file a Report of Water Right Conveyance after closing.

### 2. Irrigation Company Shares (Mutual Water Companies)
**Water shares** are shares of stock in a private mutual irrigation company that owns the underlying canal, reservoir, or pressurized secondary water system. Across Ephraim, Manti, Mt. Pleasant, Spring City, Fountain Green, Moroni, Gunnison, Nephi, Mona, Levan, Delta, and Richfield, most agricultural and pasture water is delivered through irrigation company shares.

Here is the trap that catches out-of-area buyers and agents: **irrigation company shares are personal property (corporate stock), not real estate.** They do *not* transfer automatically with a standard warranty deed. To get the water at closing, the seller must endorse the physical share certificate (or sign a lost-certificate affidavit), pay any past-due annual assessments and transfer fees, and have the irrigation company secretary issue a new certificate in the buyer's name.

## What Does a Standard Utah Domestic Well Permit Actually Cover?

When buyers ask about **Juab County domestic well permit rules** or building on five acres in Sanpete, Millard, or Sevier County, the biggest surprise is how little outside watering a standard domestic well allocation covers.

While exact acre-foot duties vary slightly by groundwater management plan, a standard Utah domestic well right for a single-family rural home is typically structured around three pieces:

1. **Domestic indoor household use (1 EDU):** Typically **0.45 acre-feet per year** for one single-family residence.
2. **Home lawn and garden irrigation:** Typically covers **0.25 acres (about 10,890 square feet)** of irrigated yard or garden around the house (often around 0.50 acre-feet, depending on the basin's irrigation duty).
3. **Stock watering:** A small allocation for a handful of horses, cattle, or family livestock (for example, enough for several horses or cows at roughly 0.028 acre-feet per animal unit).

Add those together and a standard domestic well allocation is roughly **1.0 acre-foot per year**. That is plenty of water for a family home, a quarter-acre yard, and a few horses drinking from a stock tank. **It will not irrigate four or five acres of pasture or alfalfa.** In Central Utah valleys, full-season pasture or alfalfa irrigation generally requires roughly 3 to 4 acre-feet of water *per irrigated acre*. If you want green pasture across five acres, you need irrigation company shares (or a much larger irrigation water right) on top of your household well.

## How Do Irrigation Company Shares Work in Ephraim, Manti, Mt. Pleasant, Nephi, and Mona?

Not all irrigation shares deliver the same amount of water—or deliver it the same way. When someone asks about **Sanpete County water shares price** or compares shares in Nephi, Mona, Ephraim, Manti, or Mt. Pleasant, there are four questions you have to ask before you agree on a sale price for the land:

1. **How many shares Equal One Irrigated Acre?** Every irrigation company has its own ratio and water source. In one company, a single share might reliably water one acre; in another company, you might need two or three shares per acre, or the share may be split into "A" (storage/late-season) and "B" (high-water/early-runoff) classes.
2. **High-Water Runoff vs. Late-Season Storage:** High-water shares only run during spring snowmelt (often May through June) until the mountain creeks drop. Reservoir or storage shares deliver water through July, August, and September when your pasture or hay crop actually needs it to survive the heat.
3. **Pressurized Secondary Riser vs. Open Ditch Turn:** Many systems around Ephraim, Manti, Mt. Pleasant, Spring City, and parts of Juab and Sevier Counties have converted to pressurized irrigation pipelines. If there is already a pressurized riser at your lot line, you can hook up a wheel line, pods, or sprinklers without running an electric booster pump. If the company still delivers through an open ditch on a turn schedule (including 2:00 a.m. water turns), you need to verify whether the lateral ditch connecting the main canal to your fence line is still intact and maintained.
4. **Can the Shares Move to Your Parcel?** Most irrigation companies only deliver within their service area and along specific laterals. Before buying shares separately—or assuming the seller's shares work on your lot—call the company water master or secretary to confirm that those shares can be delivered to your parcel's turnout.

## What About Haul-Water Subdivisions and Mountain Cabin Lots?

Not every Central Utah parcel can get a domestic well permit or town culinary water. In several mountain and foothill subdivisions (like gated cabin communities above Fountain Green, Mt. Pleasant, or Fairview), properties use **hauled water stored in a buried cistern** or a seasonal private association water system.

Before you buy a haul-water or seasonal-water parcel:
- Check the county's health department and building department rules. Many counties allow hauled water for a seasonal recreational cabin, but require an approved year-round potable water source for a full-time residence.
- Understand how lenders view hauled water. Most conventional, FHA, and [USDA Rural Development loans](/blog/usda-rural-development-loans-central-utah) require a continuous, potable year-round water supply that is customary for the area and accepted by the local health authority.

## The Pre-Contract Water Checklist for Central Utah Buyers and Sellers

Whether you are [buying rural property](/buying-rural), shopping for [Utah land and acreage](/land), or preparing to [sell your country property](/selling), verify these items in writing before your due-diligence deadline expires:

- **Exact Share Certificate & Company Name:** Get a copy of the stock certificate and call the irrigation company secretary to confirm the shares are in good standing and all annual assessments are paid.
- **Delivery Method on the Ground:** Walk the property to locate the pressurized riser, meter, or ditch headgate. Verify whether any private lateral line crosses a neighbor's land and whether a recorded water-line or ditch easement exists.
- **Utah Division of Water Rights Record:** For any existing or proposed well, pull the official file from the Utah Division of Water Rights to confirm the owner of record, priority date, allowed uses (domestic, stock, irrigation), and that the point of diversion matches the parcel.
- **Change Application & Well Log:** If you are buying raw land and acquiring a water right to drill a well, make sure your contract accounts for the Utah State Engineer's change-application process. If a well is already drilled, review the well driller's log and order a current pump/flow test and water quality test.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much do Sanpete County water shares cost, and how do they affect land sale price?
Irrigation share prices vary widely by company, town, and whether the water is reliable late-season storage or spring runoff only. Because many shares can only be used inside a specific company's canal or pressurized pipeline boundary, there is no single county-wide price—two parcels just a few miles apart in Ephraim, Manti, Mt. Pleasant, or Spring City can sell for tens of thousands of dollars apart depending on the exact shares included.

### What are the Juab County domestic well permit rules for building on rural acreage?
To drill a domestic well in Juab County (and across Sanpete, Millard, and Sevier Counties), you must hold an approved underground water right with the Utah Division of Water Rights (often requiring an approved change application moving an existing right to your well site), hire a licensed Utah well driller, and meet Central Utah Public Health Department septic setback and water-quality rules before the county issues a building permit.

### How many acres can you water with a standard Utah domestic well?
A standard Utah domestic well right for a single-family home typically covers indoor household use for one home, a small number of livestock, and about a quarter-acre (0.25 acres) of lawn or garden. Watering multiple acres of pasture or hay requires additional irrigation water rights or irrigation company shares.

### Do irrigation company water shares transfer automatically with the deed in Utah?
No. Shares in a mutual irrigation company are personal property (corporate stock) and must be listed on the purchase contract and transferred by endorsing the share certificate and registering the transfer with the irrigation company secretary at closing.
