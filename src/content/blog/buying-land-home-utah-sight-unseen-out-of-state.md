Buying land or a home sight-unseen in Central Utah is common among out-of-state buyers — I work with them regularly, from California, Colorado, Texas, the Pacific Northwest, and elsewhere — and it is entirely manageable when the process is set up correctly. But it requires more deliberate verification than a purchase where you can drive by and look, and skipping steps is where buyers get burned.

Here is how the process actually works, what to verify, and how to protect yourself whether you're buying a [home](/buying-rural), [vacant land](/land), or a [mountain cabin](/cabins).

## Why People Buy Sight-Unseen in Central Utah

The buyers I work with who purchase remotely fall into a few common categories:

- **Out-of-state buyers** who have identified Central Utah as the right market for affordability, acreage, or recreation but cannot easily fly in for multiple visits
- **Wasatch Front residents** buying [mountain cabin or recreational property](/cabins) in areas they have visited recreationally but not scouted as buyers
- **Investors and future retirees** securing property now for a planned move in one to five years
- **[Homesteading-minded buyers](/blog/buying-acreage-homesteading-central-utah)** who have researched Central Utah extensively online and want to lock in a property before prices shift

All of these are legitimate approaches. The key is replacing the in-person visit with equally thorough alternative verification — not skipping verification altogether.

## What Replaces an In-Person Visit

### Video Walkthroughs
I do live and recorded video walkthroughs for out-of-state buyers regularly. These are not listing-photo slideshows — they are honest, narrated tours that show you:

- **The property itself** — every room of a home, the yard, outbuildings, the driveway, the views. I talk through what I'm seeing and what I'd want to know if I were buying it.
- **The approach and road** — what the drive looks like coming in, the road surface and condition, how far from the nearest paved road, what the neighbors' properties look like
- **For land** — the actual parcel boundaries where identifiable, the terrain, the vegetation, neighboring land use, the view from different points on the property
- **What the camera doesn't show** — I narrate things like road noise, cell signal (I test it on-site), wind exposure, and anything that feels different from how the listing presents it

A video walkthrough from someone who will be honest with you is more valuable than a personal visit where you are excited and not asking the hard questions. I will tell you what concerns me, not just what looks good.

### Qualified Local Inspections
For a home purchase, a qualified local inspector is your most important protection. For rural Central Utah property, this means someone experienced with:

- **Well systems** — flow rate testing, water quality analysis, pump condition, pressure tank function. A well that "works fine" may be producing inadequate flow or poor-quality water. A generalist inspector may not test a well with the same thoroughness as a rural-experienced one.
- **Septic systems** — inspection, tank condition, drain field assessment. Septic issues can cost $10,000 to $30,000+ to resolve.
- **[Manufactured home construction](/blog/manufactured-modular-homes-central-utah-financing)** — foundation adequacy, HUD certification, title status, and condition-specific concerns that a standard inspector may not evaluate
- **Older or non-standard construction** — log homes, additions, owner-built structures, and the kinds of properties common in rural areas
- **Heating systems** — propane, wood stove, or other systems that differ from the suburban furnaces most inspectors are used to

I have inspectors I trust in this area who understand these specific property types. A $400 to $600 inspection can save you from a $50,000 mistake.

### County Records and Title Work
Before you are financially committed, the following should be confirmed through a title company experienced with rural Utah:

- **Zoning** — what the property is actually zoned for, not what the listing implies
- **Easements** — recorded access easements, utility easements, and any restrictions on the property
- **Water rights** — documented and verified through the State Engineer's records
- **Liens or encumbrances** — any outstanding claims, back taxes, or unresolved title issues
- **Boundary verification** — confirmed through a plat map or survey
- **Building permits** — whether existing structures were permitted, especially additions or outbuildings

A rural-experienced title company catches issues that a standard title search might overlook. Easement disputes, unrecorded access agreements, and water rights complications are more common in rural transactions than in suburban ones.

## Land-Specific Verification

Buying vacant [land](/land) sight-unseen carries extra risk because there is no structure to inspect and fewer reference points for value. These steps are not optional:

### Confirm Parcel Boundaries
Do not rely solely on GPS coordinates from a listing or pins dropped on Google Maps. Satellite imagery can be outdated, and fence lines do not always match legal boundaries. Get a survey or at minimum a clear county plat map that shows the exact parcel boundaries. For larger parcels, a professional survey is worth the cost — boundary disputes on rural land are more common than buyers expect.

### Verify Water Rights
In Utah, [water rights are a separate legal asset from land ownership](/buying-rural). They do not automatically transfer with the land unless specifically included in the sale. Before buying any land:

- Confirm whether water rights exist for the parcel and what type (well, irrigation shares, spring rights)
- Verify they are in good standing with the State Engineer (rights can be forfeited if not used)
- Understand the quantity and intended use — domestic, irrigation, and stock water rights serve different purposes
- If no water rights exist, determine the feasibility and cost of drilling a well (and whether well permits are available in that area)

A parcel without water rights in a semi-arid state is a fundamentally different purchase than one with confirmed water. This is one of the most important things I check for any land buyer.

### Check Access — Legally, Not Just Physically
A dirt road visible on satellite imagery might look like access to the property, but it may be a private road, a seasonal forest service road, or cross private land without a recorded easement. Verify:

- Is there a recorded legal easement providing access to the parcel?
- Is the access road county-maintained, private, or shared?
- Is access year-round or seasonal? (Many mountain and foothill roads close in winter)
- What condition is the road in, and what kind of vehicle is required?

If the only access crosses someone else's property without a recorded easement, the property is effectively landlocked for financing and practical purposes. I verify legal access before showing any property to a buyer.

### Understand What's Buildable
If you plan to build on vacant land, verify:

- **Septic feasibility** — has a perc test been done? Not all soil supports a standard septic system, and alternative systems are expensive
- **Utility availability** — how far is grid power? Is there a phone or internet connection available?
- **Building codes and permits** — what does the county require for new construction?
- **Setback requirements** — how far from property lines must structures be?
- **Zoning restrictions** — what types of structures are permitted?

A parcel that looks perfect in photos but fails a perc test or sits two miles from the nearest power line may cost $50,000 to $100,000+ in infrastructure before you can build. These are not afterthoughts — they are the first questions to answer.

## Financing Sight-Unseen Purchases

Financing works the same whether you visit in person or not — the lender evaluates the property through an independent appraisal regardless. But a few things matter more for remote buyers:

**For homes:** The appraisal and inspection are your primary sources of truth about condition and value. For [loan type comparisons in Central Utah](/blog/conventional-vs-fha-vs-usda-central-utah) — conventional, FHA, USDA — see my financing guide. USDA's 0% down option is available in most of Central Utah and can be a game-changer for out-of-state buyers who qualify.

**For land:** Land financing requires a larger down payment (typically 20-35%) and more documentation than a home purchase. Conventional land loans, seller financing, and cash are the common paths. See my [land buying guide](/land) for details.

**For cabins and second homes:** [Cabin financing](/blog/financing-cabin-second-home-utah) requires conventional loans with 10-20%+ down. USDA and FHA do not apply to non-primary residences.

**Appraisal challenges:** In rural Central Utah, comparable sales can be limited, especially for unique properties or vacant land. As a certified appraiser and REALTOR, I understand this process from the inside and can help you anticipate potential appraisal issues before they affect your financing.

## Protecting Yourself as a Remote Buyer

### Non-Negotiable Protections
- **Never waive an inspection contingency** on a sight-unseen purchase, regardless of how good the deal looks or how much pressure the seller puts on you. An inspection contingency is your right to walk away if the property has undisclosed issues. Waiving it to win a deal is the single riskiest thing a remote buyer can do.
- **Use a title company experienced with rural Utah transactions.** Standard title companies may miss rural-specific issues like unrecorded easements, water rights complications, or boundary disputes.
- **Get a written seller disclosure** and compare it against what your inspector and title company find. Discrepancies are red flags.

### Smart Practices
- **Ask for recent, dated photos or video**, not just listing photos that could be months or years old. Listing photos are marketing material — they show the property at its best. Ask what has changed since the photos were taken.
- **Check county records yourself** — many Utah counties have online GIS mapping, tax records, and property information accessible from anywhere
- **Talk to neighbors** if possible — they will tell you things the seller and listing agent may not
- **Verify insurance availability** before closing — some rural properties, especially mountain properties, can be difficult to insure. Get quotes before you are committed.
- **Visit if you can.** Even one visit before closing is better than none. A video walkthrough is valuable, but standing on the property, driving the road, and getting a feel for the area adds information that a screen cannot convey. If you can budget one trip, make it count.

## Common Sight-Unseen Mistakes

These are the mistakes I have seen out-of-state buyers make, and the ones I work to prevent:

- **Trusting satellite imagery for current conditions** — Google Earth may be 2 to 5 years old. Roads, structures, vegetation, and neighboring land use can change.
- **Assuming water is available** because the listing mentions a creek or because neighboring properties have wells. Water in Utah requires verification, not assumptions.
- **Underestimating infrastructure costs** on raw land — the purchase price is just the beginning. Well, septic, power, access improvements, and fencing can easily exceed the land cost.
- **Buying based on price alone** without understanding why a property is cheap. The cheapest parcel may lack water, access, or buildable soil.
- **Skipping the title search** or using a title company unfamiliar with rural issues

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to buy land in Utah without visiting first?
It can be, with the right verification steps — a survey or plat confirmation, water rights documentation, title work through a rural-experienced company, a perc test if you plan to build, and a video walkthrough from someone you trust. Skipping these steps is where sight-unseen land purchases go wrong.

### Do I need a special inspector for a sight-unseen rural home purchase?
Yes, ideally. A generalist home inspector may not have deep experience with well systems, septic, manufactured home construction, or older rural homes — all common in Central Utah. I recommend inspectors who understand these property types.

### Does land in Utah come with water rights automatically?
No. Water rights are a separate legal asset from land ownership in Utah and must be specifically documented and transferred. Never assume they are included in a land sale — verify through the State Engineer's records and the title company.

### How do I verify a property's value from out of state?
The lender's independent appraisal provides a professional opinion of value. Beyond that, I can give you comparative market analysis showing what similar properties have sold for. As a certified appraiser and REALTOR, I bring both perspectives to help you evaluate whether a price is fair.

### What if I can only visit once before buying?
One visit is significantly better than none. Use it to see the property in person, drive the access roads, visit the nearest town for a realistic feel for the area, and meet your inspector on-site. Do your document verification (title, water rights, county records) remotely before the visit so you can focus the trip on the things that require your physical presence.

### Can I close remotely?
Yes. Title companies routinely handle remote closings through mail or mobile notary services. Your physical presence at closing is not required.
